# Bengali and Durga Puja: do I even know what I'm celebrating?

Durga Puja is coming up and lately I keep seeing this video. There's an old grandpa who says that Durga Maa comes and gets her jewellery from the Daw house in Jorasanko, eats at Abhay Charan Mitra's house in Kumartuli and then goes to watch dancers at the Shobhabazar Rajbari. I think it's from a very old movie, but I'm not sure. Either way, I love this story.

I'm Bengali myself and I grew up celebrating Durga Puja. But I realised I never really knew what's behind it, so I decided to find out.

Old-timers say it goes like this:

- **The jewellery: the Daw house in Jorasanko.** This is the Shibkrishna Daw house in north Kolkata and their Durga puja has been going since 1840. The goddess here is known for being dressed in gold, with diamonds and emeralds said to have been brought from Europe. That's why people say this is where she comes to get dressed up.
- **The lunch: Abhay Charan Mitra's house in Kumartuli.** Kumartuli is the same neighbourhood where the idols are made. His house was known for serving gigantic sweets, which is apparently why she'd stop here to eat. Sadly, I read that this puja stopped a long time ago.
- **The music and dance: the Shobhabazar Rajbari.** This is the royal family home of Raja Nabakrishna Deb. During the puja they held all-night music programmes in their famous dancing hall (the *nach-ghar*), and that's why people say the goddess comes here to enjoy the evening.

I found this story on a bunch of heritage blogs and travel sites, but none of them say who first told it or when. They also seem to copy each other, so I can't tell if it's a really old tradition or just a story that got repeated a lot online. I'd love to find an older source. What I could confirm, in two separate places, is that the Daw house puja started in 1840. One source says Shibkrishna Daw's father started it and another names Gokul Chandra Dawn as the founder, so the details differ a little.

One more thing: all three of these are what Kolkata calls **bonedi baris**, the old aristocratic family houses that have held Durga Puja for generations. One travel writer says there are around 200 of them in the city, with pujas that are anywhere from 150 to 400 years old. I wonder how a family puja at home turned into the giant public festival we see now. That's coming up later.

*Trust check: the three-house story is a folk tale with no known origin. The 1840 date for the Daw house is confirmed in two places. The "200 houses" number is from one source.*

## So why do we celebrate it, and what actually is it?

I feel like a lot of people my age, including me, celebrate Durga Puja without knowing what it all means. It's celebrated in lots of places, not just in India. UNESCO says it's celebrated most notably in Kolkata, but also in other parts of India and among the Bengali diaspora, so there are Bengalis all over the world doing this.

UNESCO also describes it as a ten-day worship of the Hindu mother-goddess Durga. Artisans make the idols from clay taken from the Ganga, the worship begins on Mahalaya when the eyes are painted onto the idols and on the last day the idols are immersed in the river the clay came from. UNESCO says that's why the festival has come to mean a kind of "home-coming," a return to your roots.

That one word made me stop. It connects to the other idea I keep hearing, that Durga comes home like a daughter visiting her parents. I wonder if that's the same thing or two different ideas that got mixed together — I dig into that a bit more below, once I get to Mahalaya.

*Trust check: the UNESCO description is verified straight from UNESCO's own page. The "daughter coming home" idea I still need to trace to a proper source.*

## Why Kolkata, though?

It's big in other places, so why does Kolkata go so crazy for it? Here's the first piece I found. In December 2021, UNESCO added **"Durga Puja in Kolkata"** to its list of Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. It's one of 14 things from India on that list.

And here's something that surprised me. Earlier, Durga Puja in Kolkata was mostly for wealthy families, the same bonedi baris from above. Then, in the early twentieth century, the community-run **barowari** pujas started and they quickly turned it into a festival for everyone. I want to understand that shift properly — it feels like the key to why Kolkata is the way it is, but for now I've only got the broad strokes.

*Trust check: the UNESCO inscription is verified in several places, including UNESCO's own page. The barowari timeline is from one source, Wikipedia, and I want a better one before I trust the details.*

## So when is Durga Puja this year?

Most calendars I checked put the main five days of Durga Puja 2026 in West Bengal like this:

|---|---|

| Maha Shashthi | Saturday, October 17 |
| Maha Saptami | Sunday, October 18 |

One site I found had everything a day earlier, from October 16 to 20, and another site's note says some calendars do that depending on which almanac they follow. So who's right? ## Who decides which day the puja falls on?

I always heard people say "it's in the panjika." So I wondered: what is that and how does it decide?

Here's what I found. The Hindu calendar doesn't run on a 24-hour day. It runs on **tithis**, or lunar days. A tithi is the time it takes for the Moon to move 12 degrees further away from the Sun in the sky. There are 30 tithis in a lunar month, 15 while the Moon is getting brighter and 15 while it's fading.

The catch is that tithis aren't the same length. They average about 23 hours 37 minutes, but one source says they range from about 20 to 27 hours (another source gives a narrower range, so I'm not sure of the exact limits). That's why you see timings like "Ashtami ends at 10:51 AM." A tithi can start and end at any time of day, not at midnight.

So Durga Puja follows the Moon. Shashthi, Saptami, Ashtami, Navami and Dashami are the 6th to 10th tithis of the bright half of the month of Ashwin. That's also why the dates move around on the English calendar every year.

The book that lists all of this is called a **panjika** in Bengal (the same thing as a panchangam elsewhere in India). It gives each tithi's name and exactly when it ends. Belur Math says it follows the *Vishuddha Siddhanta* almanac.

Here's where it gets interesting. One source says that in Indian calendars the tithi in effect at sunrise is the tithi "of that day." Because tithis don't match days, sometimes a tithi gets counted on two days and sometimes one gets skipped. I'm not sure Durga Puja uses exactly that rule, so I won't claim it does. Also, different almanacs can calculate tithis differently, which may be why the October 16 sites disagree. I don't know which method each Bengali panjika uses.

I also don't know who actually picks between almanacs for a given puja, a committee or a priest. I'd love to find out.

*Trust check: the tithi definition (12 degrees) is confirmed in several places. The "different almanacs may disagree" part is from one calendar site's note plus the fact that dates differ across sites. The sunrise rule is from one source about Indian calendars in general, not specifically about Durga Puja.*

## Mahalaya: the morning that starts it all

Mahalaya is the first thing on the calendar and it's a day with two meanings. It's the last day of **Pitru Paksha**, the fortnight when people honour their ancestors (the ritual is called tarpan), and the day **Devi Paksha**, the fortnight of the goddess, begins. UNESCO adds that this is when the eyes get painted onto the clay idols, which is when the goddess "comes alive."

Two blogs also say Mahalaya is the day Durga starts her journey from Kailash to her maternal home. That connects back to the homecoming story from earlier and to the three-house tale I opened with.

Then there's the radio. If you grew up Bengali, you probably know the 4 AM sound of *Mahishasuramardini*:

- It was first broadcast on Akashvani (now All India Radio) in Calcutta at 4 AM on Mahalaya, **September 22, 1931**.
- Bani Kumar wrote the script, Pankaj Mullick composed the music and **Birendra Krishna Bhadra** narrated and recited verses from the *Chandi*, the same text from the Mahishasura legend above.
- It started as a live performance. It's been played from a recording since 1966.

There's a famous story that in 1976, AIR tried replacing Bhadra's voice with the actor Uttam Kumar and the backlash was so strong that the original came back. One Wikipedia page says the programme has aired every year except 1976, which doesn't quite match, so I'm not sure what exactly happened.

*Trust check: the 1931 date and the three creators are in several sources, and the 1966 recording date is in two. The 1976 story is from two sources, and the details are unclear. The Kailash-journey line is from two blogs.*

## What happens on each day?

This part is mostly from Belur Math's page on how it holds the puja. Different places do things a little differently, so this is one tradition's version.

| Day | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Shashthi** | The ritual preparations: *Kalparambha* (the resolve to do the puja properly), *Bodhan* (awakening the goddess, at dusk), *Adhivas* and *Amantran* (invoking and inviting her) |
| **Saptami** | Nine plants (the *Navapatrika*) are bathed and placed beside the idol, and the goddess is invoked into the image |
| **Ashtami** | *Kumari Puja* (a young girl worshipped as the goddess) and **Sandhi Puja**, the most important moment |
| **Navami** | *Homa*, a fire ritual |
| **Dashami** | The goddess is asked to return, the idols are immersed in the river, and people embrace each other |

**Sandhi Puja** is the one worth understanding. It happens in the last 24 minutes of Ashtami and the first 24 minutes of Navami, the "juncture" between two tithis. That's why its time changes every year. At Belur Math it was 11:43 AM to 12:31 PM in 2024, 5:43 PM to 6:31 PM in 2025 and this year it's **10:28 AM to 11:16 AM on Monday, October 19**. One site also says it's customary to light 108 lamps at this time.

Here's something that confused me. Belur Math's explanation puts *Bodhan* under Shashthi, but in its yearly schedules it appears the evening before Shashthi's main rites. I'm not sure why.

*Trust check: the day-by-day list is from Belur Math, one source. It's a religious institution describing its own tradition, so other places may vary. The Sandhi Puja times come from Belur Math's own schedules. The 108 lamps detail is from one calendar site.*

## So, do I actually know what I'm celebrating now?

More than I did a week ago, at least.

Going in, I thought this would mostly be folklore — a nice story about a goddess visiting three houses before going home. What actually surprised me is how much of Durga Puja runs on things I never thought to question: a lunar calendar made of tithis that don't even match the length of a day, a radio broadcast from 1931 that still opens the festival every single year, a 24-minute ritual window that shifts by hours depending on the year. None of that was ever explained to me growing up. I just showed up when the pandal was ready.

I think that's the real answer to why so many of us my age celebrate this without knowing it in depth, it's not that nobody told us, it's that the festival hands you the experience fully assembled. You get the dhak, the lights, the five days off, the new clothes. Nobody hands you the panjika.

What I still don't have is the thing I actually started this for — a real source for the three-house story, the one that pulled me in. Every blog repeats it, nobody traces it. Maybe that's just what oral tradition looks like once it hits the internet: the story survives, the footnote doesn't. I'm okay leaving that one open for now.

If I had to tell other Gen-Z Bengalis one thing: the festival gets a lot more interesting once you stop treating the dates and rituals as fixed facts and start asking why they land where they do. Almost everything in this post was something I'd absorbed as "just how it is" turns out most of it has a reason, a disagreement, or a gap in the record behind it.

Next puja, I want to go further back — maybe actually track down Guha-Thakurta's book, or just ask someone in my own family what they remember from before I was old enough to ask.
